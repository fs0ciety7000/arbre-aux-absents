**RECORD OF HEARING No. 2**

In the year nineteen hundred and seventy-six, on Thursday the fifth of February, at ten past two in
the afternoon, in hearing room No. 3 of the Civil Re-registration Office, at Aubrelieu,

before us, Fernand Ollier, investigating clerk, acting on the instructions of the rapporteur in
charge of the file (office 12),

there appeared **the claimant**, who states that his name is Bastien Tessonnal, born on 14 May 1910
at Hautcorvin, night foreman at the furnace hall, residing at Dalvica (Thalberie), and that he
maintains his petition of 12 January;

in the presence of **Maître Gérard Vallin**, barrister of the Aubrelieu Bar, for Mrs Colombe
Brossal, née Tessonnal, objecting party, who has lodged a list of written questions (reference
76-0012/8);

the rapporteur having forwarded three written questions on 3 February, read by us in their turn.

The claimant is informed that he may refuse to answer and that his answers will be placed on the
file.

---

**Q. (Maître Vallin)** — What did you call your sister when you were children?

**A.** — Colo. She hated it. She’d pull my hair when I said it in front of people, in the middle of
my forehead, where it stuck up.

*Maître Vallin observes that the claimant wears his hair cropped short. The claimant replies: “In
the penal colony they shaved us. I kept the habit.”*

**Q. (Maître Vallin)** — There was a cat in your mother’s shop, on the rue Haute. Its name?

**A.** — Bobine. A ginger cat that slept in the hatboxes. My mother used to say it was the only
customer in the house who never paid.

*Maître Vallin acknowledges, on behalf of his client, that the answer is correct.*

**Q. (Maître Vallin)** — You burnt yourself at the hall. When, and where?

**A.** — In 1927, at Furnace No. 2. I was seventeen. The gather rolled off the pipe onto the back
of my right hand. They put butter on it, which was stupid. My father told me a glassblower without
a burn is no glassblower.

*Invited by Maître Vallin to show his hands, the claimant rolls his sleeves up to the wrist and
refuses to take off his woollen gloves: “People looked at my hands enough over there.” Maître
Vallin asks that this be noted, and that a medical examination be ordered. Duly recorded.*

**Q. (Maître Vallin)** — The Vèze customs report of May 1938 gives the man named Tessonnal grey
eyes. Yours are brown.

**A.** — Customs men write down what they like, at night, by lantern light. And eyes darken with
age, Maître. Yours will too, you’ll see.

**Q. (Maître Vallin)** — Where were you working on the night of the blaze?

**A.** — At Furnace No. 2, until midnight, as night foreman. I never left the furnace. The fire
started in the packing store, at the far end of the hall, where there’s nothing that gets hot. Ask
the night watchman. His name was Mallet. I don’t know if he’s alive.

**Q. (Maître Vallin)** — What did you give Miss Lucie Neviot, of the refuge de l’Ourse?

**A.** — A glass tear. A drop of glass you let fall red-hot into cold water: it turns hard as
stone, except the tail, and if you break the tail it bursts into dust. Glassblowers make them for
girls. I scratched a B on it with the point of a nail. Badly. She laughed.

**Q. (the rapporteur)** — Who were your godfather and your godmother?

**A.** — My godfather, my grandfather Évariste. My godmother, my aunt Marguerite, Sister Agathe, at
the Hautcorvin hospice. She’d bring me an orange on Saint-Nicolas, and a sermon with it.

*The claimant adds nothing.*

**Q. (Maître Vallin)** — We show you the photograph published in *The Herald* of 9 September 1941
(“The Regent at Hautcorvin”). Do you recognise the piece Mr and Mrs Brossal are holding, and can you
describe it?

**A.** — The Regent’s Cup. A cup blown in two gathers, the foot applied. The engraving is done on
the copper wheel – the small one, two lines – with fine emery mixed in oil; the veins of the laurel
are gone over with the diamond point, one by one, otherwise the leaf is dead. There are forty-two
leaves in the wreath, and on the…

*The claimant breaks off. He does not look at the background of the photograph.*

**Q. (Maître Vallin)** — You had been at Kessaar since 1938. How do you know this cup, and how many
leaves it has?

**A.** — I was told about it. In the colony people talk about everything, there’s time. And I know
glass.

**Q. (the rapporteur)** — Tell us about Andrin Dalvarin.

**A.** — He was a good man. He engraved better than his mother, and his mother was the best in the
hall; she held the wheel the way you hold a child. They called us the two brothers of the Combe.
You mustn’t hold it against him that he died. You mustn’t hold his name against me. I’ve worn it
decently.

**Q. (the rapporteur)** — In November 1975 the Office wrote to “A. Dalvarin, Dalvica”, concerning
the petition of Mrs Agnès Neviot. Did you receive that letter?

**A.** — Yes. I didn’t know what to answer. So I came.

**Q. (Maître Vallin)** — What exactly are you asking for? Mr Honoré Tessonnal’s estate?

**A.** — I’m asking for my name. The rest, they can share it out however they like. I’d like to see
Agnès. Not for the inheritance. Once, sitting down, in a warm room.

*The claimant asks that his request be passed on to the rapporteur. Duly recorded.*

---

Closed at 16.25. Having heard this read over, the claimant confirms it and signs with us; Maître
Vallin signs subject to all his client’s rights.

**THE CORVINS SENTINEL** — Weekly of the upper valley — Hautcorvin — Saturday 24 January 1976
— 0.40 Fn

## THE REVENANT OF HAUTCORVIN

### A man presents himself at the Office as Honoré Tessonnal's missing son; the valley is divided

*by Isaure Brossal*

He arrived at the beginning of the month, by the pass road, with a cardboard suitcase and a suit too
big for him. On Monday 12 January he walked into the Civil Re-registration Office and said: “I am
**Bastien Tessonnal**.” Honoré Tessonnal's son, who vanished from the valley in 1938 and has been
counted among the dead ever since, asks to be given back to the living. Less than a month after his
father's death.

We met him last Saturday, 17 January, in our offices. He agreed without needing to be asked twice.
“Let them see me,” he said. “That's why I came back.” He is a thin man, with white hair cropped short
and straight black eyebrows. He speaks low, chooses his words, and throughout the interview keeps his
hands on his knees, in woollen gloves that he did not take off. From his neck hangs an engraver's
loupe on a cord. “A habit,” he says. “You pick up habits, over there.”

### “I took a dead man's name”

His account takes a few sentences, and he repeats it without changing a word. In 1944, in the Kessaar
penal colony, his comrade Andrin Dalvarin died, on 9 March. He, Bastien, was due to stand trial for
“sabotage”. He is said to have taken the dead man's name and number, and to have lived thereafter,
for twenty-four years, under that name, on the other side of the pass. “Out of fear at first. Out of
shame afterwards. You don't come back when they think you're a fire-raiser.” Why now? “The blaze is
time-barred. My father is dead. There's nobody left whom I'd hurt by coming back.” He adds, more
quietly: “And I have a daughter I've never seen. I'd like to acknowledge her before I die.”

He describes himself as “night foreman” at Furnace No. 2, the last post he held in the Vernezac hall.
He gives the names of the old hands, the order of the furnaces, the bell for the change of shift. On
the valley as it was in 1937, he did not make a single mistake in front of us.

### The old hands of the hall

We showed his photograph to three former workers of the Hautcorvin Glassworks who knew Bastien
Tessonnal before the war. The first refused to give an opinion. The second, a former glassblower at
Furnace No. 2, looked for a long time: “That's the old man's dimple all right. As for the eyes, I
can't say any more… It's a long way back, eyes.” The third shrugged: “Thirty-eight years. Who do you
recognise, after thirty-eight years?”

### A nurse from Vernezac

There is someone else in this affair, someone who had never asked anyone for anything.
**Agnès Neviot** is a nurse at the Hautcorvin hospice. She is the daughter of Lucie Neviot, who was
keeper of the Refuge de l'Ourse. In 1974 she asked the Office to give her back a father. She had
nothing but a letter from her mother.

“I haven't seen him yet,” she told us. “I don't know if I want to see him. I'd like it to be him,
that's all. That's no reason for it to be him.” She saw us together with her half-brother, **Pierre
Sarrade**, a ski instructor at Vernezac, who did see him go by. “On Saturday the 10th I saw him going
up towards the refuge, in town shoes, in snow up to his knees. He didn't ask me the way. He knew it.”

### The family keeps silent

The director of the Hautcorvin Glassworks, Bastien Tessonnal's sister, did not wish to comment. On
Thursday her barrister lodged, on behalf of the family, an objection to the petition. The Office is
examining the file; a sitting is expected in March.

We do not know who this man is. The Office will say.

---

*Editor's note. Our contributor, who writes under her maiden name, is the granddaughter of the late
Honoré Tessonnal and the daughter of the objecting party. She was determined to write this article.
The editors have read it and stand by it.*

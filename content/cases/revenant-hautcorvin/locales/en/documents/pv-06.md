**RECORD OF HEARING OF A WITNESS**

In the year nineteen hundred and seventy-six, on Monday the twenty-third of February, at five
o’clock in the afternoon, at the town hall of Vernezac, in the wedding room, the only heated room,

before us, deputy mayor, registrar, acting in execution of the letters rogatory of the Civil
Re-registration Office (file 76-0012), in the presence of Mr Fernand Ollier, investigating clerk of
the Office, who holds the pen,

there appeared **Mr Victorin Sarrade**, born on 14 January 1915 at Hautcorvin, retired sergeant of
the Vèze customs, widower of Lucie Neviot, stepfather of the joint claimant, who himself asked to
be heard, by letter of 19 February.

The witness swears to tell the truth. He states: “I’d have said it sooner if anyone had asked me.
Nobody has asked me in thirty-eight years.”

---

**Q.** — The night of 21 May 1938.

**A.** — I was twenty-three; I’d been a customs man for two years. There were two of us on patrol,
Sergeant Achille Fauvel and me, below the scree on the Pas de l’Ourse, because we’d been told the
fugitive Tessonnal would come through. He came through. We took him at the foot of the scree. I was
the one holding the lantern. I had him a step away. The man I arrested had grey eyes, so pale the
lantern shone straight through them.

**Q.** — The arrest report mentions a woman who is said to have fled.

**A.** — She didn’t flee. She turned round on the gap, with the moon behind her, and she looked at
us, and then she went. It was Lucie Neviot, the refuge keeper. I knew her by her plait. Fauvel
didn’t recognise her; he never went up to the refuge. I said nothing. I wrote “a woman”, same as
him. I’ve told no one till today.

**Q.** — Why?

**A.** — Because I was twenty-three and I already loved her. That’s no reason. It’s mine.

**Q.** — Did you know what was going on at the Pas after 1938?

**A.** — Not straight away. Later, yes. Lucie and her father went up at night with people who had
no name any more, and they came down alone. I let them do it without knowing, and afterwards I let
them do it knowing. On 12 February 1944, Jules Neviot didn’t fall. They wrote “fall”. I heard the
rifle shots from the post. After February 1944, I looked the other way. I did my round an hour
later, or an hour earlier, as she told me. She kept a notebook. I never read it.

**Q.** — You married Lucie Neviot in 1947. You did not acknowledge the child.

**A.** — She wouldn’t have it. She said the little one already had a father, and that one day she’d
be given his name back; that if I acknowledged her, that couldn’t happen any more. Fauvel was my
witness at that wedding. He never knew a thing.

**Q.** — According to some people in the village, the child is said to be the Thalbère’s daughter.

**A.** — My sister-in-law Odette Neviot always believed the little one was the Thalbère’s, because
she’d seen him go up to the refuge every Saturday in the winter of 1938. I let her believe it. It
covered for the other one. The Thalbère took the bread up; it wasn’t him in the loft.

**Q.** — Have you seen the claimant?

**A.** — Twice. The first time in June 1953, on the doorstep of the refuge, at Lucie’s. He came on
foot over the col, thin as a rake, and he gave her a piece of paper. For a moment she thought it was
the other one, with the sun behind him. Then he spoke. She cried all night. The next day she told
me: “It’s the Thalbère. He’s come back from the island. Bastien is dead.” It was Andrin Dalvarin,
the son of the woman engraver.

The second time, on 5 February, in the corridor of your Office, at Aubrelieu, when I drove the
little one there. The man in the corridor is the one from 1953. He’s aged like everybody. He has
dark eyes. The man I arrested in 1938 had grey ones.

**Q.** — Why did you ask to be heard?

**A.** — Because the little one got a glass, seventeen years ago, and she has lent it to the Office.
Because the man in the corridor stopped in front of her and said nothing. And because Lucie left me
something to do.

*The witness hands the Office a tin lozenge box, dented, tied shut with string, which he calls
“Lucie’s box”. He states that he has never opened it and that he received it from his wife a few
days before her death, on 23 January 1969, with this instruction: “The day someone asks you for the
truth, give it.” He adds: “I’m being asked.” The box is opened in his presence and inventoried by
the clerk of the Office (separate inventory record, item 13).*

---

Closed at 18.15. Having heard this read over, the witness confirms it and signs with us and with
the clerk of the Office.

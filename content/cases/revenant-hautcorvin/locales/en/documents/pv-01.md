**RECORD OF HEARING No. 1**

In the year nineteen hundred and seventy-six, on Tuesday the thirteenth of January, at half past
nine in the morning, in hearing room No. 3 of the Civil Re-registration Office, at Aubrelieu,

before us, Fernand Ollier, investigating clerk, acting under Article 4, paragraph 3, of the Act of
14 May 1973 (hearing of the claimant within three days of the lodging of the petition),

there appeared **the claimant** in petition No. 76-0012, who states that his name is Bastien
Tessonnal, born on 14 May 1910 at Hautcorvin, and that he resides at Dalvica (Thalberie).

The claimant is informed that he may refuse to answer, that his answers will be placed on the file
and that any false statement is punishable under Article 15 of the Act. He states that he has
understood.

---

**Q.** — Tell us about your childhood.

**A.** — Hautcorvin, the house on the rue Haute, over my mother’s shop. The village school, Mr
Henri Brissac, who rapped our knuckles with his ruler and read us poetry on Saturdays. On
Thursdays and in summer we’d go down to the Combe-aux-Sables, to the quarrymen, and slide down the
white sand all the way to the Orvelle. We came home white as millers. They called us the two
brothers of the Combe.

**Q.** — The two brothers?

**A.** — Me and Andrin. The son of the Thalbère woman, Danela, the engraver at the hall – she’d
been marched to the border with him in the expulsion of 1916, and she came back in 1921. My uncle
Léonce Tessonnal’s son, people said. My uncle had died in the Passes; nobody had asked his opinion.
Andrin was two years younger than me. When he came back from over there, they put him on my bench,
in Mr Brissac’s class, because he’d learnt to read in Thalbère and not in Velmarchois. Within a
year he read better than I did.

**Q.** — Your occupation?

**A.** — Apprentice at the Vernezac furnace hall at sixteen, glassblower at nineteen. Night
foreman at twenty-five. That’s what I wrote on your form: night foreman. That’s what I was when
they took me.

**Q.** — Tell us about the 1936 walkout.

**A.** — Mr Brossal had cut wages by an eighth. We laid down our pipes one Monday in June, with the
furnaces lit. You don’t do that to a furnace; we did it. I was at the front, because somebody had
to be at the front and I was the master’s son: they wouldn’t sack me. They didn’t sack me. They did
worse to me, later.

**Q.** — The night of the blaze, 27 November 1937.

**A.** — I was at the furnace until midnight, with my crew. The fire started in the store, at the
far end of the hall, where there’s nothing but straw and crates. Aimé Brossal was there that night.
I saw him cross the yard around eleven, his overcoat over his arm, heading for the store. I can’t
prove it. I’m saying it anyway: the blaze has been time-barred since 1967, so I’m told, and Mr
Brossal is dead. I gain nothing by saying it, and he has nothing left to lose.

**Q.** — What did you do next?

**A.** — Andrin came and found me in the bucket chain, before the pumps arrived. He said: “They’re
saying it was you. Get away before they come up.” I went up to the refuge de l’Ourse. The keeper
hid me. Lucie Neviot – the keeper of the Ourse, as they called her in the village. She was
twenty-three. Six months in the loft of the refuge, the whole winter. Andrin came up on Saturdays
with bread, tobacco and the paper. He pretended to be courting her, for the Guards’ benefit. He
did it badly, on purpose.

**Q.** — Your arrest.

**A.** — The night of 21 May 1938, at the Pas de l’Ourse. We meant to cross into Thalberie. I’ll
tell you the path, since you ask me to prove who I am. From the refuge, an hour. You go left of the
spring, never right, on the right it slides. At the third bend, the iron cross: you keep it on
your left hand and you don’t touch it, it brings bad luck. After the cross, a slab that rings
hollow when you step on it; you have to stride over it, at night the sound carries right down to
the post. Then the scree, which you take along the top, against the rock face, for thirty paces.
The gap is just after. The boundary stone is cut with a V on the Velmarchois side, and on the other
side with a Thalbère sign nobody has ever been able to read. The customs men were waiting below the
scree. They had a lantern.

**Q.** — Were you alone?

**A.** — Don’t ask me who was with me. She’s dead. She has the right to be left in peace.

*The clerk does not press the point.*

**Q.** — Your detention.

**A.** — Kessaar, 3 June 1938. Prisoner number 0214. Cell 14. On my own three years, roughly. In
October 1941 they put Andrin in the cell with me. Andrin Dalvarin, number 1171. He’d been struck
off over some engraving business – “insulting the Regent”, the list said. He stayed with me two and
a half years. We had time to tell each other everything. He talked, I slept; I talked, he
remembered.

**Q.** — What happened in 1944?

**A.** — In February the chief warder read me a paper: my file was going over to “sabotage”, I’d be
tried at Aubrelieu in April. We knew what a trial in April meant, at Kessaar. On 9 March Andrin died
in the infirmary, of his chest. I was on water duty at the infirmary that evening. We wore our
number tag round the neck, on a lace. I took his and left him mine. At night, no one was looking.
The next day 0214 was dead and 1171 was alive. Nobody asked anything. At Kessaar they didn’t look
at faces, they looked at numbers.

**Q.** — And then?

**A.** — Released on 11 April 1952, under his name. I went to his mother’s, at Dalvica, over the
col. Where else would I go? She kept me. I was re-registered under his name at Vernezac in 1953, in
front of two workmen from the hall who didn’t look at me either. Twenty-four years in Thalberie
under the name of Andrin Dalvarin. I scratched glass for a living, at Brénica. A glassblower
without a furnace does what he can.

**Q.** — Why did you not apply for re-registration in 1953, under your own name?

**A.** — In 1953 I had a blaze on my back, and a father alive. I didn’t dare become myself again
while my father lived. He died on 19 December. I have no one left to shame.

**Q.** — Why now?

**A.** — I have a daughter. Agnès Neviot, at Vernezac. She was born in February 1939; I was already
over there. She asked for a father to be given back to her; they told her he was dead. I’m not
dead. I want to acknowledge her, before you, with my name. That’s all I’m asking.

*Informed by us of the terms of the will of Mr Honoré Tessonnal, and of the existence of a sealed
letter addressed “to the one of my children who has not borne my name”, the claimant states:*

**A.** — It’s for me. I lost the name in 1938.

*The claimant adds nothing.*

---

Closed at 11.50. Having heard this read over, the claimant confirms it and signs with us.

*Clerk’s observation.* The claimant answered without notes, without hesitating over a single date,
and without once asking for a question to be repeated. He spoke about the man named Andrin Dalvarin
for longer than about himself.

**RECORD OF HEARING OF A WITNESS**

In the year nineteen hundred and seventy-six, on Monday the ninth of February, at three o’clock in
the afternoon, at Hautcorvin, at the home of the person appearing, the Tessonnal house, rue Haute,
in the study of the late Mr Honoré Tessonnal,

before us, Fernand Ollier, investigating clerk, acting under letters rogatory from the rapporteur
in charge of the file (office 12),

there appeared **Mrs Colombe Brossal**, née Tessonnal on 23 July 1906 at Hautcorvin, Director of
the Hautcorvin Glassworks since 1946, widow of Mr Aimé Brossal, objecting party,

assisted by Maître Gérard Vallin, her counsel; in the presence of her son, Mr Hugues Brossal, whom
she asked to keep at her side.

---

**Q.** — Describe your brother, Bastien Tessonnal, as you knew him.

**A.** — He had our mother’s eyes. Grey, almost white in the sun; in photographs you’d think he had
no eyes at all. A cowlick in the middle of his forehead, like our father, that nothing would flatten,
neither water nor brush. And he laughed with his mouth open, because of his tooth. He’d chipped it
on a blowpipe at eighteen; he never would have it mended. He said it was his glassblower’s medal,
along with the hand.

**Q.** — The hand?

**A.** — The right one. Burnt across the back, shiny as glass. In 1927.

**Q.** — Have you seen the claimant?

**A.** — His photograph. I didn’t look at it long enough to know who he is. Long enough to know who
he isn’t.

**Q.** — What did your brother call you?

**A.** — Colo. No one else has ever called me that. I’m told the claimant knows it. All Hautcorvin
knew it, in 1920.

*The person appearing hands the clerk a pencilled note on squared paper, folded in four, signed
“B.”, which she states she received from her brother on 1 December 1937, by a messenger she does
not name. She states that she hands it over “for the handwriting, since you have experts”. The
note is entered on the file as item 11.*

**A.** — I kept it thirty-eight years in my missal. Give it back to me when you’ve finished.

**Q.** — On the night of 27 November 1937, your brother was accused of setting fire to the hall.

**A.** — I never believed my brother set the fire. I won’t say why.

**Q.** — Was the hall’s night watchman, Toussaint Mallet, questioned at the time?

**A.** — Both his hands were burnt; no one heard him. After that no one ever heard from him again,
on anything. He died in 1955. His rounds register should be in the archives with the other books
from the hall: I deposited them all in 1968. I haven’t read it again.

**Q.** — Your mother, Eugénie Ravenel. Your father’s will speaks of “my living children”. Do you
know whether he had any children other than you and your brother?

**A.** — Mama knew, about the Thalbère woman. She used to say it was no reason to be rude.

**Q.** — What do you mean?

**A.** — What I said. My father had only two children. My brother lost his name in 1938; the
envelope is for him. He is dead. Let the notary keep it.

*In the margin of the preceding answer, at the request of Mr Hugues Brossal, the following is
noted:*

*Mr Hugues Brossal, engineer at the Glassworks, intervenes to ask that his mother “tell
everything”. He states that there exists “a sealed envelope, deposited by my mother with Maître
Escande in 1958”, the contents of which he does not know. The person appearing replies: “That
envelope is mine. It is no concern of the Office.” Informed by us that the Office may require it to
be produced, she replies: “Then require it.” She refuses to say more. Maître Vallin reserves all
rights.*

**Q.** — Would you agree to be confronted with the claimant?

**A.** — No. If he is my brother, he knows why. If he isn’t, I have nothing to say to him.

---

Closed at 16.40. Having heard this read over, the person appearing confirms it and signs with us,
as does Maître Vallin. Mr Hugues Brossal signs for the note that concerns him.

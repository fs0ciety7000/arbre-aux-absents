**THE AUBRELIEU COURIER** — Daily — Saturday 18 May 1889 — Price per copy: 5 sous

## OUR WALKS AROUND THE GRAND EXHIBITION

### The light of Tennok

*In the Fire Arts Gallery, the crowd queues before a lighthouse that does not light.*

To come near it, one must wait one's turn behind a tasselled rope, between the enamelled stoves of
Corbeval & Co. and the Saint-Ormel faience; yet nobody, since the opening of the
Grand Exhibition, has complained. In the centre of the gallery, under a glass case, the
**Hautcorvin Glassworks** are showing the first-order lens which will carry, from this autumn, the
light of the Cap de Tennok, at the very tip of Ostrie. It is a beehive of glass taller than two men,
made of cut rings and prisms, which gathers the light from the great glass roof and throws it back in
flashes. The ladies look at themselves in it; the children look in it for the sea.

**Mr Évariste Tessonnal, director** of the Glassworks, stands beside his work from morning till night
and answers every question, his spectacles pushed up on his forehead. Two years of work, he tells us,
one of them spent polishing by hand, “because a machine does not know when to stop”. The Tennok lens
will leave again in crates, by road to Port-Sénane, then by boat to the cape: “We blew it in the
mountains so that it might see the sea.”

One also notices, at the foot of the plinth, the firm's presentation casket, its gold lines as fine
as a hair: it was **gilded by Mrs Tessonnal herself**, the daughter of a Hautcorvin gilder, who runs
the gilding workshop and is said to be the finest glass gilder in the upper valley. Their son, a boy
of eight, never leaves the stand; he knows the number of the rings better than the attendants do.

The jury for the glass class begins its visits on Monday. One can hardly see who might dispute the
highest award with a light such as this.

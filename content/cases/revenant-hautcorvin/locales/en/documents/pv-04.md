**RECORD OF HEARING OF THE JOINT CLAIMANT**

In the year nineteen hundred and seventy-six, on Wednesday the eleventh of February, at one
o’clock in the afternoon, at Hautcorvin, at the Hautcorvin hospice, in the linen room, the only room
free at the change of shift,

before us, Fernand Ollier, investigating clerk, acting under letters rogatory from the rapporteur
in charge of the joined files,

there appeared **Mrs Agnès Neviot**, born on 14 February 1939 at Vernezac, nurse at the hospice,
residing at Vernezac, claimant in file 74-0962. She asked to be heard in her uniform, between two
shifts, “so as not to lose half a day”.

---

**Q.** — What did your mother tell you?

**A.** — My father’s name, and his trade. Only once, in January 1969, a fortnight before she died.
Before that she said nothing. When I asked, she’d say: “Your father was a good man, and you’ll be
told when we’re allowed.” She didn’t wait to be allowed.

*The person appearing produces her mother’s letter, from Lucie Neviot, of January 1969, which she
had mentioned in her petition. A certified copy is placed on the file; the original is returned to
her.*

**Q.** — Did your stepfather know your father?

**A.** — My stepfather, Victorin Sarrade, has never been willing to talk about those years. He was a
customs officer all his life; he knows how to keep quiet better than anyone. He keeps Lucie’s box in
his wardrobe, under his shirts. He doesn’t open it. He says it isn’t his.

**Q.** — The box?

**A.** — A lozenge box, a tin one. My mother had it at the refuge de l’Ourse, then at home. That’s
what we call it at home, Lucie’s box, as if my mother were still a young girl.

**Q.** — Have you seen the claimant?

**A.** — Once, on 5 February, in your corridor, at Aubrelieu. My stepfather had driven me there to
sign for the joining of the files. He was coming out of the hearing room, behind Mrs Brossal’s
barrister and yourself. He stopped. He didn’t say anything to me. Nor I to him. My stepfather took
me by the arm and we left.

**Q.** — Do you recognise him as your father?

**A.** — I’ve never seen my father. My mother took me once, when I was twelve, to the library at
Hautcorvin. She asked for an old newspaper and showed me a man raising his arm among the workmen,
in front of the door of the hall. She said: “Look at his eyes.” He doesn’t have the eyes in the
photograph.

*The person appearing is silent for a moment.*

**A.** — I don’t care. If he’s lying, he’s lying for me. I don’t know yet whether that pleases me.

**Q.** — Has the claimant been to Vernezac?

**A.** — My brother Pierre Sarrade saw him going up towards the refuge de l’Ourse on Saturday
10 January, alone, in town shoes, in weather when nobody goes up. He came down at nightfall. He
spoke to no one. Pierre is an instructor; he’s in the habit of counting the ones who go up and the
ones who come down.

**Q.** — Do you have any other documents?

**A.** — A glass. I received it on 14 February 1959, my twentieth birthday, by post, with no
sender’s name. An engraved glass, with the Pas, a she-bear and her cub, and underneath: “To the
glassblower’s daughter”. I didn’t know who was sending it. Perhaps I know now. I’m lending it to
you. Don’t break it.

*The person appearing hands over an engraved glass tumbler, wrapped in a tea towel, with the
original postal packaging. It is listed separately and entered on the file.*

**Q.** — What are you asking for?

**A.** — The same thing as in 1974. A name on the empty line. The rest – the inheritance, the house
– I haven’t asked for it and I don’t want it. I’d like it to be the right name, that’s all.

---

Closed at 14.05. Having heard this read over, the person appearing confirms it and signs with us.

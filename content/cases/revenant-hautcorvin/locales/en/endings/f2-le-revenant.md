## [commun] The sitting

On Monday 15 March 1976, the Re-registration Commission sat in Aubrelieu on joined files
Nos 76-0012 and 74-0962. The table of descent held: twenty-eight slots, each resting on two
documents. The report concluded that the petition should be granted. The Commission concurred.

“Bastien Tessonnal, glassblower, born in Hautcorvin on 14 May 1910, living” was re-registered in
the Hautcorvin register. At the bar, he acknowledged as his daughter Agnès Neviot, born in Vernezac
on 14 February 1939. He signed the acknowledgement with a good straight T, which he had taken two
months to learn. Agnès signed beneath it and asked him nothing. In February she had told the clerk
that this man did not have the eyes in the photograph, and that it made no difference to her.

The decision had its price, set down in black and white in the report, and the Commission read it
aloud because the law required it to. Andrin Dalvarin, glass engraver, prisoner number 1171, son of
Danela Dalvarin, ceased to be registered. His re-registration of 3 June 1953, in Vernezac, was
annulled. He gave up his own name and his mother's. In the margin, the register recorded: “Absent
by choice, 1976”.

## [s1] The Fire of 1937

To the first question the report answered: Aimé Brossal, company secretary of the Glassworks, died
1958. It cited the report of the L'Égide assessor, which placed the seat of the fire far from the
furnaces, and the log of Toussaint Mallet, night watchman, whose torn-out page had left its trace
pressed into the next one: “23.10 — M. B. at the packing store”.

Colombe Brossal had not appeared in February, when her envelope was opened. She came to the
sitting. The chairman read out the inventory of the 1958 envelope: Rider No. 3 to the L'Égide
policy, two petrol chits initialled “A. B.”, a receipt from the Vèze Garage dated 26 November 1937,
Mallet's letter. When he had finished, she stood, identified each item as one she had put under
seal herself, and said: “My brother did not set the fire. I have known it for eighteen years.” She
said it looking at the chairman, and not at the man who now bore her brother's name.

The name he had taken came out of it cleared. He had not set the fire either; no one could have
held it against him, and no one did.

## [s2] The Pas de l'Ourse

In July, a plaque of engraved glass was set into the stone to the right of the door of the Refuge de
l'Ourse. Victorin Sarrade fixed it himself, with four brass screws:

> Through this pass, from 1938 to 1952, Lucie and Jules Neviot brought one hundred and forty-two of
> the absent across.

The engraving was the work of a very sure hand, too sure for a glassblower. Victorin noticed. He was
in the habit of looking the other way; he looked the other way.

## [s3] Honoré's envelope

The sealed envelope of the will was addressed “to whichever of my children did not bear my name”.
The report established that Andrin Dalvarin was Honoré's son; but since that morning Andrin Dalvarin
had been registered nowhere. Maître Escande read the address three times over. Of Honoré Tessonnal's
two children on the register, only one had gone without his name for years: a man struck off the
registers from 1938 to 1976. He handed him the envelope as Bastien.

The claimant opened it on the bench in the corridor. The letter ran to a single page.

> Andrin,
>
> If Maître Escande gives you this, the Office has spoken, and I shall have been wrong to keep silent
> to the very end. I never knew how to call you “my son” except in glass. I said it in florins, in
> the workshop's pay book, from 1912 to 1938, and your mother was kind enough to pretend she did not
> understand.
>
> Léonce carried what I did not carry. He was your godfather, he let people talk, and he died
> without once complaining of it.
>
> In 1952 it was through you that I learned of Bastien's death. You did not know whom you were
> telling. I did not reply. I wrote “to my living children”, and it was you I was thinking of; I
> could not bring myself to write your name anywhere but here.
>
> Honoré Tessonnal

He had taken his brother's name to give a child back her father. He learned, on the same day and
too late, that he had had a father too. He would inherit from Honoré all the same, as Bastien, from
a man he had believed to be his uncle. He folded the letter in four and put it in the inside pocket
of the suit that was too big for him, next to the magnifying glass.

## [s4] The Regent's Cup

The Regent's Cup left the exhibits store and entered the Museum of the Fire Arts in Aubrelieu, with
a new label:

> Andrin Dalvarin, 1941. In the laurel: B · T · 38.

At the opening of the display, a man in a suit too big for him stood bent over the laurel for a long
time. The gallery attendant asked him whether he was family. He said he was the brother.

## [s1-non-prouve] The name of a fire-raiser

The report said nothing of the night of 27 to 28 November 1937. Removal List No. 9 therefore went on
saying what it had said since 1938: “criminal blaze at the Hautcorvin Glassworks”. The offence was
time-barred; it would never be tried, and so never cleared. By signing, the claimant had taken the
name with everything written on it.

In Hautcorvin, on the place des Verriers, there were people who crossed the street. He did not
protest. He knew better than anyone who had set the fire, and he also knew he had no document to
prove it.

## [conclusion] Conclusion

In the register, three notes: “Registered living, 1976”; “Absent by choice, 1976”; “Acknowledged by
the claimant, 1976”. Each fits on a single line, and one of them is false.

Since April, Bastien Tessonnal has lived in a room above the Auberge des Guides, in Vernezac. Agnès
comes up to see him on Sundays. They never talk about Kessaar.

In office 12 at the Office in Aubrelieu, the rapporteur has kept in a drawer, in a folder with no
reference, what he did not attach to the report: a slot card, the seventeenth, with a profile
photograph taken on 9 January 1976 and a name written in pencil. He has shown it to no one. Nor has
he destroyed it.

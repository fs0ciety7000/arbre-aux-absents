**THE AUBRELIEU COURIER** — Daily — Tuesday 12 March 1968 — 0.35 Fn

## THE REGENCY PAPERS (3)

### The papermaker and the grey booklets

*Since 1 January the archives of the Regency have been open to historians. Our journalist Paul Marsal has spent ten weeks in them. Third part of his inquiry: the man who made the paper.*

By **Paul Marsal**

Since the opening of the **Regency Papers**, much has been said of those who signed: the Regent, his ministers, the Commissioners of Registers. Less has been said of those who supplied the means to sign. A removal, before it is a crime, is a sheet of paper. Someone made it.

The **grey booklet**, the control book that more than six hundred thousand Velmarchois had to produce on every demand between 1938 and 1952, came from a single house: the Varenque Paper Mills, of Moulinvert. The boxes of the Ministry of the Interior, which I was able to consult, contain the initial order of May 1938 (four hundred thousand copies), its eleven renewals, the technical correspondence on the grain of the paper, the “pearl grey” tint and the watermark, a fern, the same one that won the house its gold medal of 1889. Every document is countersigned by the Director of the Paper Mills, **Mr Gaspard Varenque**. None bears the trace of an objection.

### A sister

The reader must know one thing here which I do not want him to learn from others: by my marriage, I have entered the family I am writing about. I draw no authority from it. I draw from it only the obligation to write what I found, without cutting anything out.

Here is what I found. In October 1941 the Guard of Order broke up a forged-papers workshop at the Moulin de la Sorbe, the “Fern” network. It arrested a young woman watermark engraver, employed at the Moulinvert Paper Mills: **Odile Varenque**, thirty-three, the director’s youngest sister. She was struck off, deported to Kessaar, declared dead in 1943. The investigation file of the Registers Office was not among the boxes opened to historians: it belongs to the collections to which the law still reserves access for the families.

*The Herald* of 21 October 1941 writes that the police traced the workshop to the mill thanks to “a personal piece of information”. It adds that the management of the Varenque Paper Mills “has given the inquiry its full assistance”. Who, in 1941, could know that an engraver came home in the evening with her hands burnt by acid? Who could know that paper was missing from the Moulinvert storerooms? Those who saw her every day. Her family. Her employer.

I do not say that Gaspard Varenque gave up his sister. I say that I have found no one else who could have done it so easily, and that he alone could say otherwise.

### A silence

He has never said it. Tried in 1953 at the Papermakers’ Trial and acquitted for want of evidence, Mr Varenque refused for three months to answer on his sister’s arrest. He spoke, in his defence, only one sentence, which the court shorthand record has preserved: “I will not defend myself with the dead.” One may hear it as a scruple. One may also hear it as a confession.

I asked Mr Varenque, who lives in retirement at Moulinvert, to see me. He sent word that he “had nothing to add to the silence of 1953”. Nor was I able to question his brother-in-law, the former Commissioner of Registers for the province, Mr Marcel Daubry, who left the country in April 1952 and of whom all trace has since been lost.

**THE AUBRELIEU COURIER** — Daily — Wednesday 13 May 1953 — 0.25 Fn

## PAPERMAKERS’ TRIAL: GASPARD VARENQUE ACQUITTED

### The Special Court finds “no evidence”; the accused did not say a word in his own defence

*After three months of hearings, two sentences of detention, one of national indignity and one acquittal. The Moulinvert industrialist leaves the law courts alone, to whistles and a little applause.*

The **Papermakers’ Trial** ended last night, at ten past nine, before the Special Court of the Republic sitting in the Aubrelieu law courts. The trial, which opened on 9 February, as is known, was judging the heads of the four houses that supplied the Regency, from 1938 to 1952, with the paper for its registers, its *Bulletin of Order*, its police record cards and its all too notorious **grey booklets**.

Mr Anatole Ferrou, formerly a director of the Estuary Paper Mills, is sentenced to ten years’ detention; Mr Félicien Mazure, of the Grisenoue Mills, to six years; Mr Henri Dorsal, of the National Printing Office of Order, to national indignity. **Mr Gaspard Varenque, Director of the Varenque Paper Mills**, of Moulinvert, is acquitted.

### A silence

This verdict, the only one of the day to raise cries in the courtroom, comes as no surprise to anyone who has followed the proceedings. Against Mr Varenque the prosecution could produce only what everyone knew: that his house had supplied the paper for the grey booklets, on the State’s order, at the market price; that his brother-in-law, the former Commissioner of Registers, now a fugitive, signed the purchase orders; that his own sister had been arrested in 1941, deported to Kessaar and declared dead in 1943. It could prove neither illicit enrichment, nor denunciation, nor zeal.

But nor did it obtain anything from the accused. For three months Mr Varenque sat upright, hands folded on the rail, and answered only questions of civil status and of figures. Questioned on his sister’s arrest, he kept silent. Questioned on irregularities in the inventories of his storerooms between 1944 and 1952, he kept silent. Pressed by the Advocate General to explain himself “at least before the families of the absent”, he at last spoke the only sentence anyone heard him say in his defence: “**I will not defend myself with the dead.**”

His counsel, Maître Fernand Desaulnes, did not plead innocence. He pleaded the empty file. The court followed him.

### On the way out

Mr Varenque left the law courts by the great staircase, without waiting for the car he was offered. He was alone; his children had not appeared at the hearing. A few whistles, a little applause, including, it seemed to us, that of several Moulinvert workmen who had come in on the morning ferry.

We asked him whether he would resume the management of his house. He looked at us as one looks at a badly cut sheet of paper, and said: “Tomorrow morning. The work doesn’t do itself.”

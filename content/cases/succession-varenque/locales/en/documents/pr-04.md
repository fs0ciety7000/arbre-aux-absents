**THE VELMARCHE EYE** — Illustrated weekly — No. 832 — Saturday 13 April 1918 — 10 sous

## THEY HAVE FALLEN

### Our dead of the Col de Corvenne, week of 17 to 23 March

*Each week, from the communications of the Ministry of War and of the families, the editors publish the names of Velmarchois soldiers fallen before the enemy. Families are kindly asked to send us portraits, which will appear as space allows.*

**Bertaux (Émile)**, 22, of Saint-Ormel, corporal, 4th of the Line, fell on 18 March at the redoute du Bec. — **Corbinel (Jean-Marie)**, 31, of Hautcorvin, mountain guide, sergeant in the Chasseurs of the Passes, fell on 19 March. — **Dauzère (Pierre)**, 19, of Grisenoue, private, 4th of the Line, fell on 19 March. — **Ferrand (Louis)**, 27, of Port-Sénane, seaman attached to the mountain batteries, fell on 21 March.

### Lucien Varenque, the poster artist of “The Washerwoman”

**Lucien Varenque**, killed on 19 March at the **Col de Corvenne** at the age of forty-two, called for a column of his own, and our readers will forgive us for it. There is not a Velmarchois who has not seen his posters. There are few who know his name.

Born at Moulinvert in 1875, the second son of Mr Anselme Varenque, the papermaker, and brother of Mr Augustin Varenque, Deputy for the Orvelle, he turned down the paper trade for painting, and easel painting for the street. His studio in the rue des Tanneurs, in Aubrelieu, was for fifteen years the merriest and the untidiest in the city. Out of it came **“The Washerwoman”** for the Mérande Soapworks (1905), the young woman with rolled-up sleeves who has been laughing on every wall in the country for thirteen years and whom our soldiers, we are told, cut out of the papers to nail up in their dugouts; then *Le Golfe d’Ambre*, for the Orvelle Packet Company, *Christmas at Belaube’s* and a hundred others. He signed L.V., small, in a corner, as one signs a friendly note.

In April 1916, at forty, an age at which nothing was asked of him, he enlisted as a **war painter**. He was posted to the camouflage section at the pass, where his work consisted, he said in a letter to a friend on our staff, “of painting rocks on canvas so that the enemy shoots at my rocks rather than at our people; it’s the first time anyone has paid me to be a realist”. He was killed in broad daylight while repainting a battery that the snow had made too visible.

His comrades buried him near the fortin des Corneilles, under a cross he had painted himself, the day before, for someone else.

*A friend of the artist is preparing, for after the war, a retrospective of his work. Anyone who owns drawings, sketches or posters signed L.V. is invited to make themselves known at our offices.*

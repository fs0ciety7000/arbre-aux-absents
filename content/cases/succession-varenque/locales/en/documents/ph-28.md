## THE AUBRELIEU COURIER — Thursday 22 November 1973 — 0.35 Fn

### The National Archives enter the age of calculation

**The LX-2 Reader, designed by the Velmarche Calculating Company, searches an index of several
hundred thousand documents in a matter of seconds.**

At the Palais des Chartes, among the eighteenth-century panelling and the busts of archivists, a
machine is purring. The **LX-2 Reader**, inaugurated on Tuesday by Mr Hautfeuille, Director of the
National Archives, is the first enquiry terminal to be installed in a reading room anywhere in the
Republic. It consults the Central Indexed Collection, a magnetic tape on which the Archives’
indexers have entered, over the past eighteen months, the names, places and subjects of nearly four
hundred thousand documents.

“It does not read for you,” warned Miss Claude Sauge, the woman engineer from the CVC who designed
the apparatus. “It tells you where to look. If you ask it something it does not know, it replies
that it has nothing. We argued for a long time to make sure it does not reply anything else.”

The machine’s first user will be the Civil Re-registration Office, set up in May, whose rapporteurs
must determine, document by document, the identity of the “absent” of the Regency. Documents
flagged by the Reader are fetched from the stacks or reproduced on microfiche by the conservation
workshop, whose work was presented to the guests by Miss Lise Daubry, conservator: “A microfiche
does not replace an original. It simply means we need not lose it twice.”

Mr Hautfeuille concluded by recalling that in Velmarche “people were long erased with paper; it is
only right that a machine should help to find them again”.

In the reading room, around a console with a green screen and a keyboard. On the left, an elderly
man with white hair, spectacles, three-piece suit, hands behind his back. In the centre, bent over
the keyboard, a woman of about thirty-five with very short hair, a polo neck, a slide rule in her
breast pocket; she points at the screen and looks at the photographer, her left eye turned slightly
outwards. On the right, a woman of about thirty in a grey workshop smock and white cotton gloves,
curly light-brown hair held back by a wide fabric headband across her forehead, thick eyebrows; she
holds a microfiche up to the light.

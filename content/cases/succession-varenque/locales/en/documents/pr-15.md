**THE AUBRELIEU COURIER** — Daily — Friday 14 January 1966 — 0.35 Fn

## THE AVIATOR VICTOR SAUGE CRASHES IN THE CORVINS

### Wreck of the mail plane found by the Hautcorvin guides; the funeral was held yesterday in Aubrelieu

*The pilot of the first flight over the Mer Grise was still flying the upper valley mail run at fifty-nine. He died at the controls, in Sunday’s storm.*

HAUTCORVIN, 12 January. — Since Monday no one had any hope left, and they were right. The wreck of the Aéro-Velmarche mail plane that went missing on Sunday 9 January between Aubrelieu and Hautcorvin was sighted late on Tuesday morning by a roped party of guides, at an altitude of two thousand two hundred metres, on the north face of the **Monts Corvins**, below the Col de Corvenne. The pilot, alone on board, had been killed outright. He was **the aviator Victor Sauge**, fifty-nine, the company’s chief pilot.

According to the first findings of the board of inquiry, the machine, a twin-engined mail transport, was caught in the snowstorm that broke over the upper Orvelle valley on Sunday afternoon. The last radio message, received at 15.40 by the Plaine-aux-Saules tower, said only: “Icing. Going down to look.” The mail, four bags, was found intact a few metres from the wreck and brought down to Hautcorvin yesterday by the guides, who insisted on delivering it themselves.

### Thirty-five years of sky

Our older readers will remember the young man with the dimples who, in July 1931, linked Aubrelieu to Kessaar in a canvas biplane, and whom the *Courier* put on its front page. Victor Sauge never left Aéro-Velmarche. Mail pilot, instructor, then chief pilot, he trained two generations of airmen and turned down, we are told, a desk in the management three times. “A desk,” he used to say, “never takes off.” He liked to tell people he was the son of a captain lost at sea; his comrades did not believe a word of it and loved him all the more.

### The funeral

The funeral was held yesterday, Thursday, in Aubrelieu, before a crowd of airmen, postal workers and artists from the Opera, where **his widow, Mrs Ninon Sauge**, has been **costume maker** for more than thirty years. An Aéro-Velmarche aeroplane flew over the cemetery, dipping its wings.

Besides his widow, the deceased leaves **his daughter, Miss Claude Sauge**, **a woman engineer** at the Velmarche Calculating Company, whom our readers may have seen on television last year presenting the new CVC machines. She thanked those present in a few words: “My father used to say you never fall, you just land somewhere else. He has landed somewhere else.”

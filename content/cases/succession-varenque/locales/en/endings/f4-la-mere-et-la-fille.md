title: "Mother and Daughter"

## [commun] The sitting

On 15 November 1974, the Re-registration Commission settled the table of descent for file
No. 74-0318, then read aloud something that was not on the agenda: the death of Odile Varenque,
entered in the Kessaar committal register on 14 February 1943, was false. Odile Varenque had been
living in Vellaak since 1946, under the name of Nora Ilvess, photographer.

The Commission set aside the death certificate, re-registered Odile Varenque as living, and Orrin
Kessvel posthumously. It restored Lise Daubry's parentage: born on Kessaar on 3 March 1942, to Odile
Varenque and Orrin Kessvel. The legacy was divided among the established heirs: Blanche Daubry,
Selda Varenque, Armand Varenque, Hélène Marsal, Lise Daubry, Tavik Varenque and Odile Varenque, to
whom her aunt thus left, by a thirty-year detour, her share of Les Tilleuls.

## [la-salle-de-lecture] The reading room

On 9 December, at nine o'clock, a woman of sixty-six in an Ostrian jersey presented herself at the
front desk of the National Archives. She had a white forelock, a camera on a strap over her
shoulder and a scar on the back of her right hand. She asked to consult the Kessaar committal
register, which is a restricted collection. The duty archivist reminded her that a request by name
was required. She filled in the slip, like everyone else, and paused a moment before writing the
name of the person concerned, because she had had no use for it for twenty-eight years. Then she
wrote: *Odile Varenque*.

The register was brought to her at table 4 in the reading room, near the Reader's console. She read
her own death certificate. Then she asked, politely, whether someone might be sent for from the
conservation workshop, on a question of paper.

Lise Daubry came up from the workshop, in a grey overall and cotton gloves, a headband in her hair.
They looked at each other. There was, according to the archivist, neither a cry nor a tear. Lise
took off her headband. Odile said: “This register is our paper. I know the grain.” Lise said: “I
know. All the Regency's paper came from us.” They sat side by side until closing time, at six
o'clock, talking about watermarks, in low voices, as one should in a reading room.

## [S2] The undated paper

The report also established that from 1944 to 1952 Gaspard Varenque had supplied the network with
the paper for its forged papers. Odile had known since 1946: it was on that paper that she wrote to
her aunt. She had never written to her brother to thank him. He died three years too soon for her to
change her mind.

## [S3] The Washerwoman

Claude Sauge typed “Lucien Varenque” on the Reader's keyboard and learnt that she was descended from
a poster. The acknowledgement of 1917 having been upheld, she was added to the heirs. She hung *The
Washerwoman* above the console, a few steps from table 4.

## [S4] The lifeboat plaque

At Tennok, Tavik Varenque had the lifeboat plaque engraved: “Aldrik Varenque, coxswain, died saving
his son”. Then he wrote to “Aunt Nora” to ask whether he ought to call her something else from now
on. She answered no.

## [S5] The informer's name

The public report named Marcel Daubry as the author of the 1941 denunciation. Blanche Daubry,
seventy-three years old, asked to see the report, read it without spectacles, then asked Lise to
stay her daughter all the same. Lise stayed. In the spring, Blanche wrote a letter to Vellaak. No
one knows what it said. It is known that there was an answer.

## [S6] The last letter

Between two sheets of the herbarium, an envelope with no reference was waiting for “the
rapporteur”. *If you are reading this, you have found two documents. That is all I wanted.*

## [fin] End

Odile Varenque has gone back to Vellaak, where her studio still bears the sign *Ilvess —
photography*. She would not change it: “Ilvess is what I became. Varenque is what I am. You can be
both in a register now.”
